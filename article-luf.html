What Is the LUF and Why Do Lower Frequencies Sometimes Disappear?

The Lowest Usable Frequency helps explain why lower HF frequencies can become difficult to use even when propagation is still possible. Absorption, atmospheric noise and the required signal-to-noise ratio all play an important role.

LUF HF Propagation DX

Amateur radio operators often notice an interesting behaviour on HF: a higher band may still provide readable signals while a lower band becomes noisy, weak or apparently unusable. At first this can seem surprising. If a lower frequency can propagate over a long distance, why would it suddenly become difficult to use?

One concept that helps explain this situation is the Lowest Usable Frequency, or LUF. Unlike the MUF, which is associated with the upper frequency limit of a propagation path, the LUF describes a lower practical limit. Below that range, absorption, noise and other factors can reduce the signal-to-noise ratio enough to make communication unreliable.

What does LUF actually mean?

The LUF is not simply the lowest frequency that can physically propagate through the ionosphere. Lower-frequency signals can often continue to propagate in some form. The practical question is whether the received signal remains strong and clear enough for the communication system being used.

For a particular path, the LUF represents the approximate lower frequency boundary at which the required communication quality can be achieved under the prevailing conditions. Below that frequency, absorption and noise may become sufficiently important that the signal is no longer usable for the intended purpose.

The important point: LUF is a practical communication limit, not a physical wall. A frequency below the LUF may still carry radio energy, but the received signal may no longer have enough quality for reliable communication.

Why can lower frequencies become difficult?

One of the main factors is ionospheric absorption. The ionosphere does not simply reflect or refract HF signals without loss. Depending on the conditions, energy can be absorbed as the signal passes through ionospheric regions.

This effect can become particularly important at lower HF frequencies. During periods of increased ionization, absorption can change significantly and may reduce the strength of signals travelling along a particular path.

The result can be counterintuitive. A lower frequency may have a propagation path available in principle, yet the signal arriving at the receiver may be too weak relative to the background noise to provide useful communication.

Noise is part of the equation

Signal strength alone does not determine whether a frequency is usable. The receiver also has to deal with noise.

Atmospheric noise, electrical interference and local man-made noise can all reduce the effective signal-to-noise ratio. Lower HF frequencies can be especially affected by atmospheric noise, including noise associated with thunderstorms and lightning over large geographic areas.

This means that a signal does not necessarily need to become extremely weak before communication becomes difficult. If the noise floor rises at the same time, the difference between the desired signal and the background can become too small for comfortable reception.

Think in terms of signal-to-noise ratio: a usable signal is not simply a strong signal. It is a signal that is sufficiently strong compared with the noise and interference surrounding it.

LUF changes with the propagation path

Just as the MUF depends on the path between two locations, the LUF also depends on the path and its conditions. There is no single LUF that applies to the entire planet at a particular moment.

A path crossing regions with different ionospheric conditions can behave differently from another path. Latitude, local time, season, solar activity and geomagnetic conditions can all influence the propagation environment.

This is one reason why a band can sound perfectly usable in one direction while another path on the same frequency is much more difficult.

What happens below the LUF?

When an operating frequency falls below the useful range for a particular path, the signal may become progressively less reliable. Absorption can reduce its strength, while noise may increase the difficulty of detecting it.

There is not necessarily a sudden moment when the signal disappears. In many situations the transition is gradual. The signal may become weaker, noisier and more difficult to copy until communication eventually becomes impractical.

This is important because the phrase “the band is closed” can sometimes oversimplify what is actually happening. The band may still contain signals, but the propagation conditions may no longer provide enough margin for the desired communication.

LUF and MUF work together

LUF becomes especially useful when considered together with MUF. The MUF describes an upper practical boundary for a propagation path, while the LUF describes a lower practical boundary.

Between these limits is a region where communication may be possible. The size and position of that usable frequency range change with time and propagation conditions.

This gives us a useful way to think about HF propagation. Instead of imagining that a band is simply open or closed, we can think about whether the operating frequency falls inside a useful propagation window for the particular path we are interested in.

LUF and MUF define different sides of the picture. MUF helps explain why a frequency may be too high for the path. LUF helps explain why a frequency may be too low because absorption and noise make the signal insufficiently usable.

Why the time of day matters

The ionosphere changes continuously as the Earth rotates and solar radiation changes. The balance between ionization, absorption and propagation therefore changes with local time.

A frequency that provides a useful path during one part of the day may become less attractive later. Lower bands often become increasingly important as higher-frequency propagation weakens, but the lower frequencies are not immune to absorption and noise.

This is why experienced operators often move between bands rather than remaining fixed on one frequency. The propagation window is constantly changing.

A practical example

Imagine a long-distance HF path during a period of increased ionospheric activity. A higher band may be working well because the ionosphere can support that frequency over the required path.

On a lower band, however, the same path may experience greater absorption and a higher noise background. The receiver may still detect the signal, but copying it reliably could be difficult.

Moving slightly higher in frequency may therefore improve the signal-to-noise ratio and make the contact easier, even though the frequency is higher. This is one of the practical situations where the concept of LUF becomes useful.

LUF is not a fixed number

It is tempting to look for a single LUF value for a band or a location, but that would give a false sense of precision. The usable lower limit depends on the propagation path, the required communication quality and the conditions at that time.

Different modes can also tolerate different signal-to-noise conditions. A weak-signal digital mode may successfully decode a signal that would be difficult to copy by ear, while a voice contact may require a much more comfortable signal margin.

For this reason, propagation predictions should be treated as useful guidance rather than an absolute guarantee of what the receiver will hear.

How can the LUF help at the station?

The practical value of LUF is that it helps explain why simply going lower in frequency is not always the solution when a higher band becomes difficult.

If a propagation tool indicates that a particular path has a relatively high LUF, moving to a much lower frequency may not improve the situation. Absorption and noise could make the lower band less useful.

Listening across several bands provides the best practical complement to propagation information. The operator can compare signal strength, noise levels and actual readability rather than relying on a single number.

The useful frequency range

When LUF and MUF are considered together, HF propagation becomes easier to visualize. There is a lower practical boundary and an upper practical boundary, with a potentially useful range between them.

That range is not fixed. It moves as the ionosphere changes. Sometimes it may be broad enough to provide several good band choices. At other times it may become narrower and leave the operator with fewer practical options.

Understanding this changing window is far more useful than simply memorizing that one band is “good” and another is “bad.”
