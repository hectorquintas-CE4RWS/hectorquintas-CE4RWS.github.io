Why Doesn't a Perfect 1:1 SWR Always Mean a Good Antenna?

A 1:1 SWR is often treated as the ultimate goal of antenna adjustment. But SWR only tells us how well the antenna system is matched to the feed line. It does not, by itself, tell us how efficiently the antenna radiates.

Antennas SWR RF

Few numbers in amateur radio are as familiar as SWR. We adjust an antenna, connect an SWR meter, and watch the reading move toward 1:1. When it finally reaches that value, it is tempting to conclude that the antenna is working perfectly.

The problem is that SWR does not measure radiation efficiency. It measures the relationship between the impedance presented by the antenna system and the characteristic impedance of the transmission line, normally 50 ohms in amateur radio stations.

A perfect match can therefore exist in an antenna system that is not particularly efficient at radiating power.

What does 1:1 SWR actually tell us?

When an antenna system presents an impedance close to 50 ohms with a small reactive component, the transmitter sees a good match. Very little power is reflected back toward the transmitter, and the SWR approaches 1:1.

This is useful information. A low SWR can indicate that the transmitter is operating into a suitable load and that the feed line is not carrying a large reflected component. It is an important measurement when setting up and checking an antenna system.

But there is a crucial distinction: matching is not the same thing as radiation efficiency.

A 1:1 SWR means that the transmitter sees a well-matched load. It does not mean that all of the transmitter's power is being converted into useful electromagnetic radiation.

Where can the power go?

Every real antenna system has losses. Some occur in the antenna itself, some in the feed line, some in connectors and other components, and some can occur in an impedance-matching network.

Consider a hypothetical antenna system that has significant resistive losses. Those losses can appear as part of the total impedance seen by the transmitter. If the system is adjusted so that the resulting impedance is close to 50 ohms, the SWR can become excellent even though a portion of the transmitter's power is being dissipated as heat rather than radiated.

From the transmitter's perspective, the system may look perfectly normal. From the perspective of the distant receiving station, however, the situation can be very different.

Radiation resistance and loss resistance

One useful way to understand this is to separate the resistive part of an antenna's impedance conceptually into radiation resistance and loss resistance.

Radiation resistance represents the part of the input resistance associated with electromagnetic radiation. Loss resistance represents power that is dissipated rather than radiated.

If an antenna has a relatively small radiation resistance and comparatively large losses, the total resistance can still produce a convenient impedance for the transmitter. The SWR may be low, but the antenna can have poor efficiency.

This is particularly important with electrically short antennas. When an antenna is physically small compared with the wavelength, its radiation resistance can become very low. The losses associated with conductors, loading coils, traps, connections and the surrounding environment can then become significant compared with the useful radiation resistance.

An antenna tuner can make the distinction even clearer

An antenna tuner can transform a difficult antenna impedance into a value that the transmitter can handle easily. This can be extremely useful in practical amateur radio.

But the tuner does not automatically make the antenna more efficient. It changes the impedance relationship between the transmitter and the antenna system. The antenna may still have substantial losses.

This is why a station can show a beautiful 1:1 SWR after tuning and still produce a relatively weak signal compared with another antenna receiving the same amount of transmitter power.

Why SWR is still important

None of this means that SWR is unimportant. Quite the opposite. SWR is one of the most useful measurements available to the amateur radio operator.

A high SWR can indicate an unsuitable load, a problem with the antenna, a feed line issue, a connector problem or an operating frequency outside the useful range of the antenna. It can also increase losses in some feed-line systems and may cause some transmitters to reduce power or protect their final amplifier.

The important point is simply that SWR should be interpreted together with other information rather than treated as a complete measurement of antenna performance.

What should we look at besides SWR?

A more complete evaluation considers the antenna's radiation pattern, efficiency, bandwidth, physical dimensions, height above ground, polarization, feed-line losses and the intended operating frequency.

Measurements of impedance and SWR tell us about the electrical relationship between the antenna system and the feed line. Radiation measurements and field-strength comparisons tell us much more about where the energy is actually going.

Even a simple on-air comparison can reveal information that an SWR meter cannot. If two antennas have similar SWR but one consistently produces stronger received reports under comparable conditions, their radiation efficiency or radiation pattern may be quite different.

The practical lesson

The goal of an antenna system is not to produce the lowest possible SWR. The goal is to transfer useful RF power into an antenna system that radiates effectively in the directions required by the communication objective.

A low SWR is therefore best understood as one piece of the puzzle. It tells us that the impedance relationship is favourable, but it does not tell us the entire story of antenna performance.

The next time an antenna produces a perfect 1:1 reading, enjoy the result—but do not stop measuring there. Ask the more important question: where is the power actually going?
