What Does an Antenna Analyzer Really Tell You?

An antenna analyzer can reveal much more than a single SWR reading. By measuring impedance across frequency, it can help the radio amateur understand resonance, resistance, reactance, bandwidth and problems in the antenna system.

Antenna Analyzer SWR Impedance

An antenna analyzer is one of the most useful diagnostic instruments available to the amateur radio operator. Unlike a simple SWR meter, which normally gives a measurement at the operating frequency, an analyzer can sweep across a range of frequencies and show how the antenna system changes.

Depending on the instrument, it can display values such as SWR, impedance, resistance and reactance. Some modern instruments can also display Smith charts and other graphical representations of the measured system.

The real value of an analyzer is therefore not simply finding the lowest SWR. It is helping us understand why the antenna behaves the way it does.

What does an analyzer actually measure?

An antenna analyzer measures the electrical behaviour presented at its measurement port. In a typical amateur radio application, the instrument is connected to the antenna system and sends a small RF signal through the system while measuring the response.

From those measurements, the instrument can determine or calculate parameters such as impedance and SWR over the selected frequency range.

The measurement is normally made at the point where the analyzer is connected. This detail is extremely important because the feed line between the analyzer and the antenna is itself part of the electrical system.

An antenna analyzer tells you what the RF system looks like from the measurement point. It does not automatically tell you the complete performance of the antenna in free space.

Resistance and reactance

Impedance is commonly represented as two components: resistance and reactance.

Resistance represents the part of the impedance associated with real power. Reactance represents energy that is alternately stored and returned by electric or magnetic fields within the system.

An antenna analyzer can show both values, which is much more informative than looking only at SWR.

For example, two different antenna systems might both show an SWR close to 2:1, but their resistance and reactance could be very different. Knowing those values can help determine what is happening electrically.

Finding resonance

One of the most common uses of an analyzer is locating an antenna's resonant frequency.

In a simple antenna system, resonance occurs when the reactive component of the input impedance approaches zero. The impedance is then predominantly resistive.

However, resonance does not automatically mean that the antenna presents 50 ohms or that it is highly efficient. An antenna can be resonant at an impedance very different from the characteristic impedance of the feed line.

This is another reason why resonance and SWR should not be treated as the same thing.

Why the lowest SWR is not always the most useful point

It is tempting to sweep an antenna and simply search for the frequency where the SWR reaches its minimum.

That can be useful, but the lowest SWR does not necessarily represent the best operating point for every purpose.

The antenna may have its lowest SWR at a frequency where the radiation efficiency, radiation pattern or intended operating range is not ideal. The analyzer should therefore be used together with an understanding of the antenna design and the communication objective.

Understanding reactance

Reactance is particularly useful when adjusting an antenna.

If the analyzer shows a positive reactance, the system is behaving inductively at the measurement frequency. If it shows a negative reactance, the system is behaving capacitively.

As the antenna is adjusted, observing how the reactance changes can provide a much clearer indication of what is happening than simply watching SWR move up and down.

This can be especially useful when adjusting wire antennas, dipoles and other resonant structures.

What a frequency sweep reveals

A sweep across a frequency range can reveal the shape of the antenna's response.

The operator may see one or more resonant points, changes in impedance, increasing SWR toward the edges of the useful range and possible unexpected behaviour caused by traps, matching networks or other components.

This information can help determine whether an antenna is behaving as expected and whether its practical bandwidth matches the intended operating range.

The feed line can change what you see

One of the most important limitations of antenna measurements is the feed line between the analyzer and the antenna.

If the antenna is not matched to the transmission line, the impedance seen at the analyzer can change with feed-line length because the transmission line transforms the load impedance according to its electrical length and characteristic impedance.

The analyzer may therefore show a different impedance at the shack than the impedance that exists directly at the antenna feed point.

This is not a measurement error. It is a real property of transmission lines.

Why calibration matters

Vector network analyzers and more advanced antenna analyzers can require calibration to establish the measurement reference plane accurately.

Calibration compensates for systematic effects associated with the measurement path and allows the instrument to make more accurate measurements at the desired reference point.

Even simple analyzers should be connected carefully, with good connectors and a sensible measurement setup. Loose connections, adapters and poor cables can introduce errors or unexpected results.

An analyzer cannot measure everything

An analyzer measures electrical characteristics. It does not directly measure how much RF energy the antenna radiates into a particular direction.

It cannot, by itself, tell you the complete radiation pattern, propagation conditions or how well the antenna will perform on a particular DX path.

An antenna can show an attractive impedance curve while still having significant losses or an inconvenient radiation pattern.

This is why antenna analysis should combine measurements with knowledge of the antenna's physical design and its intended use.

Using an analyzer to troubleshoot a station

An analyzer can be extremely useful when something suddenly changes.

A shifted resonant frequency may indicate a change in antenna dimensions, a damaged connection, moisture, a failed component or a change in the surrounding environment.

An unexpected impedance curve can also reveal problems in traps, matching networks, connectors or feed lines.

Comparing a current sweep with a previous known-good measurement can be particularly valuable. The analyzer becomes not only a tuning instrument but also a diagnostic tool.

The practical lesson

An antenna analyzer is most useful when it is treated as an instrument for understanding an RF system rather than simply as a device for finding the lowest SWR.

SWR, resistance, reactance, impedance and frequency response together provide a much clearer picture of what the antenna and feed system are doing.

The analyzer does not replace antenna theory, and it does not directly measure radiation efficiency or propagation performance. What it does provide is something extremely valuable: measurable electrical information that allows us to connect theory with the real antenna in front of us.

Once that perspective is adopted, the antenna analyzer becomes one of the most useful pieces of equipment on the amateur radio workbench.
