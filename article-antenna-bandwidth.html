What Does Antenna Bandwidth Really Mean?

Every antenna has a practical frequency range in which its electrical characteristics remain suitable for a particular purpose. Understanding bandwidth helps explain why an antenna can perform well on one part of a band and behave very differently only a small distance away.

Antennas Bandwidth SWR

Antenna bandwidth is often described simply as the range of frequencies over which an antenna can be used. That definition is useful, but it leaves out an important question: used for what?

An antenna does not suddenly stop working when the frequency moves outside its preferred range. Its impedance changes, its SWR may increase, its losses can become more significant, and its radiation characteristics can change. The practical bandwidth therefore depends on the performance criterion being considered.

This is why two antennas with apparently similar dimensions can have very different useful bandwidths.

What is antenna bandwidth?

In practical amateur radio, antenna bandwidth usually refers to the frequency range over which the antenna meets a specified requirement. That requirement might be an SWR limit, acceptable efficiency, useful radiation performance or some combination of these factors.

For example, an operator may define the usable bandwidth as the range over which the SWR remains below a chosen value. Another antenna might be evaluated according to efficiency or impedance stability.

There is therefore no single universal bandwidth number that describes every aspect of an antenna's performance.

Antenna bandwidth is not simply the frequency range in which an antenna “works.” It is the range over which the antenna satisfies a defined performance requirement.

Resonance and bandwidth are not the same thing

Resonance occurs when the reactive components of an antenna system cancel at a particular frequency, leaving an impedance that is predominantly resistive.

A resonant frequency is therefore a specific point, while bandwidth describes a range around that point.

An antenna can have one clearly defined resonant frequency and still be useful over a wider range of frequencies. Conversely, an antenna can have several resonances and still not provide equally good performance across the entire frequency range.

Why does SWR change with frequency?

An antenna's impedance is frequency-dependent. As the operating frequency changes, the relationship between its resistance and reactance also changes.

If an antenna is adjusted for a good match at one frequency, moving away from that frequency will usually change the impedance seen by the feed line. The SWR can therefore rise as the frequency moves farther from the preferred operating point.

The rate at which this happens depends strongly on the antenna design. Some antennas maintain a useful match across a relatively broad frequency range, while others are much more narrowband.

Why are some antennas narrowband?

Electrically small antennas often have narrower bandwidth because their impedance can change rapidly with frequency and because their reactive components can be large compared with the resistive component.

Loading coils and other reactive components can make compact antennas resonant, but they can also contribute to a system in which the useful frequency range is relatively narrow.

This does not make a narrowband antenna a bad antenna. A narrowband design can be perfectly appropriate when the station is intended to operate around a particular frequency range.

What makes an antenna broadband?

Antenna geometry has a major influence on bandwidth. Designs with larger physical dimensions relative to wavelength and less extreme reactive behaviour can often maintain useful electrical characteristics over a wider frequency range.

Some antenna designs intentionally use structures that support operation over multiple bands or a broad frequency range. Other systems rely on traps, matching networks or active switching to cover different portions of the spectrum.

Broadband does not automatically mean more efficient, however. Bandwidth, efficiency, gain, radiation pattern and physical size are separate characteristics that must be considered together.

Bandwidth and antenna efficiency

There can be an important relationship between physical size, efficiency and bandwidth.

Very compact antennas can be made resonant over a desired frequency range, but achieving both high efficiency and wide bandwidth becomes increasingly difficult as the antenna becomes electrically small.

This is one reason physically larger antennas often provide advantages that cannot be reproduced simply by adding an antenna tuner.

A tuner can transform the impedance presented to the transmitter, but it does not automatically make a narrowband antenna physically broadband or remove losses from the antenna system.

A tuner can change the SWR without changing the antenna

This is an important practical distinction. Suppose an antenna has a high SWR at a particular frequency. An antenna tuner can transform the impedance so that the transmitter sees a much better match.

The transmitter may then operate comfortably at a low SWR, but the underlying antenna has not necessarily become a more efficient radiator at that frequency.

The tuner has changed the impedance relationship seen by the transmitter. It has not changed the physical dimensions or fundamental radiation characteristics of the antenna.

Bandwidth can be different on different bands

A multiband antenna may have very different bandwidth characteristics on each amateur band.

An antenna can have a relatively broad usable range on one band and a much narrower response on another. Resonances, traps, dimensions, feed-point impedance and matching networks can all contribute to this behaviour.

This is why an SWR sweep across the actual operating bands can be much more informative than checking the antenna at only one frequency.

What should the radio amateur measure?

An antenna analyser or vector network analyser can provide a much more complete picture than a single SWR reading. Sweeping the antenna across a frequency range allows the operator to see how resistance, reactance and SWR change.

This can reveal the resonant frequency, the approximate usable bandwidth and whether the antenna behaves differently from what was expected.

Measurements should still be interpreted carefully. The feed line, connectors, baluns, transformers and installation environment can all influence what the instrument sees.

The practical lesson

Antenna bandwidth is not simply a number printed in a specification sheet. It is a description of how an antenna behaves across frequency under a defined set of conditions.

A narrowband antenna can be highly effective when used within its intended range. A broadband antenna can offer greater flexibility, but bandwidth alone does not guarantee high efficiency or good radiation in the desired direction.

The most useful way to think about bandwidth is therefore in combination with the rest of the antenna system: resonance, impedance, SWR, efficiency, radiation pattern and operating purpose.

Once these characteristics are considered together, an antenna analyser becomes more than a device for finding the lowest SWR. It becomes a tool for understanding how the entire antenna system behaves across the spectrum.
